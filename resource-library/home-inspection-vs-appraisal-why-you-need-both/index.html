Home Inspection vs. Appraisal: Why You Need Both Before Closing

A side-by-side breakdown of what home inspectors and appraisers actually look for during escrow, who they work for, and how each protects you before closing.

Rakesh Jain
Senior Loan Officer & Partner • NMLS #240594 • Nov 2, 2026 • 5 min read

One of the most common questions buyers ask me during escrow is: "Why do I need both an appraisal and a home inspection? Can we eliminate one to save time and money?"

The short answer is no.

2. The Appraisal: Protecting the Lender’s Investment

When you finance a home, the property acts as collateral for the loan. The lender orders an appraisal to ensure the home is actually worth the agreed-upon purchase price.

  • The Scope: The appraiser conducts a 20-to-30-minute visual walk-through, measuring dimensions, confirming bed/bath counts, assessing general condition, and noting visible health and safety concerns (such as peeling lead paint, missing handrails, or water damage).
  • The Valuation: Most of an appraiser’s work happens after leaving the home, researching recent comparable sales ("comps") in the immediate neighborhood to establish fair market value.
  • Health & Safety Check: For government-backed loans (FHA, VA), appraisers verify specific minimum property requirements (such as functional heating and sound roofing), but they do not run diagnostic tests on equipment.
  • Appraisal Shortfall Leverage: If the appraisal comes in below the purchase price, your contingency gives you and your agent critical leverage to renegotiate the contract price down to appraised value, ask the seller to split the difference, or cancel escrow with your deposit protected.

3. The Home Inspection: Protecting Your Safety and Wallet

A home inspector is hired by you to uncover what lies beneath the surface. Their job is to identify deferred maintenance, safety hazards, aging mechanical systems, and code violations before your inspection contingency period expires.

  • The Scope: A thorough inspection takes 2 to 4 hours. Inspectors climb on roofs, squeeze into attics, crawl beneath foundations, test every electrical outlet, run appliances, and check plumbing water pressure.
  • The Report: You receive an exhaustive, itemized report—often 30 to 50+ pages with high-resolution photos and repair severity ratings.
  • Zero Focus on Value: A home inspector does not care if the home is priced at $500,000 or $1,500,000; their sole concern is whether the structure and systems are safe and functional.
  • Repair & Credit Negotiations: The inspection report serves as your direct negotiation tool. If significant issues are found, your agent can submit a Request for Repair asking the seller to complete certified repairs before closing or credit you funds toward closing costs.

4. Attendance: Should You Be There?

  • Home Inspection — Absolutely Attend: Block out time to attend the final 30 to 60 minutes of the home inspection. Walking the property alongside your inspector provides context that written reports simply cannot convey. They will physically point out shutoff valves, demonstrate how to operate HVAC systems, and distinguish minor cosmetic quirks from critical structural defects.
  • Appraisal Inspection — Skip It: There is no need for buyers to attend the appraisal. Appraisers require space to take uninterrupted measurements and notes. Let your listing or buyer agent supply any comparable sales data or upgrades directly to the appraiser if requested.

5. Key Questions to Ask Your Home Inspector

When you walk the property during the debrief, use these targeted questions to get clear, actionable takeaways:

  1. 1
    "What are the major red-flag items versus routine maintenance?"

    Reports can look intimidating with 40+ line items. This question separates immediate structural, safety, or water intrusion issues from simple DIY maintenance fixes.

  2. 2
    "How much remaining life is in the big-ticket systems?"

    Ask specifically about the estimated age and condition of the roof, HVAC compressor/furnace, water heater, and electrical panel.

  3. 3
    "Do any findings warrant a specialist’s evaluation?"

    If the inspector spots potential foundation settling, sewer line issues, or roofing deterioration, find out if you should bring in a structural engineer, licensed roofer, or sewer scope specialist before removing contingencies.

  4. 4
    "Where are the main emergency shutoffs?"

    Have the inspector show you the exact locations of the main water shutoff valve, electrical breaker panel, and gas meter shutoff.

  5. 5
    "If this were your home, what would you fix before moving in?"

    This prompts the inspector to share practical, real-world perspective on safety and priority repairs without professional jargon.

The Bottom Line

An appraisal tells you what the home is worth today, while a home inspection reveals what it will cost you tomorrow. Skipping the home inspection leaves you blind to latent structural issues; skipping the appraisal means no mortgage approval. Working together, they give you the complete picture needed to navigate your contingency timelines and close with confidence.
